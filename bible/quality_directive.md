# NON-NEGOTIABLE VISUAL QUALITY DIRECTIVE

*Authority #2 in the order of authority (after the KJV text, before the Master Visual Bible).*

This project must NOT look like generic AI-generated content.

The goal is footage that feels like it came from a high-budget serialized anime production: deliberate cinematography, character acting, environmental storytelling and visual continuity.

Never optimize for "AI-looking spectacle." Optimize for intentional filmmaking.

## 1. Avoid generic AI aesthetics
Do NOT produce:
- generic AI Bible imagery or slideshow-style compositions
- static talking characters
- characters staring into the camera without motivation
- repetitive close-ups or excessive slow motion
- random particle effects, excessive glowing light or artificial lens flares
- plastic-looking skin, waxy faces or perfectly symmetrical faces
- overly smooth animation, floating objects or morphing anatomy
- inconsistent facial features, or clothing that changes between shots
- random environmental details or excessive depth-of-field blur
- fake-looking volumetric fog
- generic "epic fantasy" imagery or stock AI cinematic compositions
- characters posing instead of acting
- unnecessary camera movement or constant dramatic zooms
- visual effects added merely because they look impressive

If an effect does not serve the story, remove it.

## 2. Cinematography must have purpose
Every shot must have a reason for its camera position, focal length, composition, movement, lighting, depth and subject placement. Do not move the camera randomly. Use cinematic grammar:
- **Establishing shot:** introduce a new environment or establish scale.
- **Wide shot:** geography, relationships, isolation, scale.
- **Medium shot:** interactions and physical action.
- **Close-up:** emotional reactions or important details.
- **Extreme close-up:** sparingly, for major dramatic moments.
- **Over-the-shoulder:** meaningful interpersonal exchanges.
- **Tracking shot:** following a character through an environment.
- **Crane / aerial:** reveal scale or transition between locations.
- **Locked-off:** when stillness creates tension.

Camera movement should communicate something.

## 3. Character acting
Characters must behave like animated film characters, not mannequins. Every character has eye direction, intentional facial expression, body language, subtle breathing, natural weight shifts, purposeful gestures, and reactions to events, objects and other characters. Characters do not simply stand in place while talking.
- **Fear:** posture, breathing, eye movement, hesitation, hand movement, physical distance.
- **Grief:** not just crying. Lowered posture, slower movement, avoiding eye contact, trembling hands, silence, restrained expression.

Prefer subtle acting over exaggerated AI facial expressions.

## 4. Environmental storytelling
The environment communicates without narration. Eden is not just a "beautiful forest": it has diverse vegetation, flowing water, animal life, enormous trees, natural sunlight, insects, distant mountains, mist in valleys, footprints, wind moving leaves and subtle environmental motion. The world should feel physically inhabited.

Ancient settlements have mud-brick architecture, stone, woven fabrics, pottery, livestock, fire, tools, carts, agricultural activity, realistic terrain and period-appropriate materials. Avoid empty environments.

## 5. Imperfect human detail
Not every character is a perfect anime model. Show believable variation: different facial structures, asymmetry, realistic skin texture, imperfect hair, different body proportions, age-appropriate wrinkles, dirt and dust, travel weathering, clothing wear, sweat when appropriate and realistic posture.

## 6. Clothing
Clothing is historically coherent: simple, functional, culturally coherent and appropriate to social position. Avoid medieval European clothing, modern fantasy robes, superhero costumes, elaborate fantasy armor, modern footwear, modern hairstyles, unnecessary jewelry and video-game costumes. Travelers look traveled. The wealthy visibly differ from shepherds, servants, prisoners and kings.

## 7. Anime style
The anime influence comes from character design, expressive animation, dramatic composition, controlled stylization, cinematic action, facial acting, color scripting and visual storytelling. It must NOT look like anime memes, chibi, generic AI anime portraits, video-game cutscenes or low-budget fan animation. Think prestige animated series, authored by a professional studio.

## 8. Color scripting
Not every scene is equally bright and saturated. Use contrast intentionally:
- **Creation:** deep blacks → brilliant white → increasingly rich natural colors.
- **Eden:** warm, luminous, harmonious.
- **The Fall:** colder shadows and visual tension.
- **Cain and Abel:** earthier, harsher.
- **The Flood:** dark skies, gray water, desaturated.
- **Abraham:** warm desert tones, deep night skies.
- **Sodom:** oppressive heat, ominous.
- **Joseph:** strong contrast between Canaan, slavery, prison and Egyptian wealth.

## 9. Lighting
Use believable sources: sunlight, moonlight, fire, torches, oil lamps, lightning, and divine light only when explicitly appropriate. No constant glowing rim light, and no "epic golden glow" as a default. Divine phenomena feel exceptional precisely because ordinary scenes are grounded.

## 10. Motion
Motion has physical weight. No floating characters, sliding feet, rubbery limbs, weightless running, unnatural hair movement, objects changing shape or impossible camera physics.
- Feet interact with the ground.
- Hands actually contact what they pick up.
- Animals move according to their anatomy.
- Rain affects characters and environments.
- Wind moves clothing, hair, vegetation, dust and smoke consistently.

## 11. Action sequences
No generic anime fights. Every action sequence has geography, clear objectives, understandable movement, physical consequences, character motivation, escalating tension and controlled camera movement. The audience always knows: Who is where? What are they trying to do? What changed? What is at stake?

## 12. Visual continuity
Continuity takes priority over spectacle. Maintain face, hair, clothing, age, props, injuries, environment, time of day, weather and geographic orientation. Check the Character Bible and the previous episode before generating every episode.

## 13. Shot variety
Within a one-minute episode, deliberately vary wide establishing shots, mediums, close-ups, environmental inserts, over-the-shoulder shots, tracking, low and high angles, POV and detail shots, but only when motivated. Never cut just to create artificial movement.

## 14. Transitions
Prefer natural transitions: match cuts, environmental wipes, movement-based transitions, fades, darkness, light, smoke, water, clouds, time-lapse and geographic transitions. Avoid flashy TikTok transitions, digital glitches, zoom transitions and AI morphing transitions.

## 15. Sound design
Sound makes the footage physically real: wind, footsteps, cloth, insects, birds, animals, water, fire, distant voices, wood, stone, metal, breathing and ambience. Silence matters. Don't put music under every second, and use silence before major events.

## 16. Music
Music supports the emotional arc rather than constantly announcing "epic." Use orchestral arrangements, ancient and world-influenced instrumentation, restrained percussion, atmospheric textures and thematic motifs (Creation, Eden, Abraham, Jacob, Joseph, Covenant), reused so the series feels like one production.

## 17. Narration
A serious storyteller. Not robotic, not a trailer voice, not constant intensity, not artificial enthusiasm. Emotion follows the story: Creation is awe, Eden peace, the Fall dread, the Flood terror, Abraham mystery, Joseph drama. Do not shout Scripture.

## 18. Dialogue
Characters speak naturally while preserving KJV wording. They do not stare into the camera. When someone speaks, give them a reason to look somewhere, allow physical movement, let others react, use pauses and interact with the environment. Dialogue is part of the scene, not voiceover pasted onto an image.

## 19. The shot test
Before finalizing any shot, check each of these: **Story** (does it communicate something?), **Character** (is anyone acting?), **Camera** (does the position serve the moment?), **Environment** (does it feel physically real?), **Continuity** (does it match?), **Physics** (does the motion make sense?), **Visual quality** (is it fit for a professional series?).

**AI-slop test:** would a viewer immediately recognize this as generic AI footage? If YES, REDESIGN THE SHOT.

## 20. Higgsfield prompting standard
Never write only "Epic cinematic anime scene of Noah's Ark." Describe the exact character, age, appearance, clothing, location, weather, physical action, emotional state, camera position and movement, composition, light source, environmental motion, depth, animation quality and continuity requirements, as if instructing a professional animation cinematographer.

## 21. Negative quality filter
Where appropriate, include constraints equivalent to: *Avoid generic AI aesthetic, plastic skin, waxy faces, malformed hands, inconsistent anatomy, floating objects, morphing faces, duplicated people, random costumes, modern objects, fantasy armor, excessive glow, excessive lens flare, oversaturated colors, static posing, unnatural facial expressions, weightless movement, videogame-cutscene appearance, slideshow composition, stock AI imagery, repetitive camera movement.* The negative prompt never overwhelms the creative prompt; the positive description is always the primary instruction.

## 22. The golden rule
The audience should forget the footage was generated by AI and think: *"I'm watching an anime."*
- Quality over speed.
- Consistency over spectacle.
- Storytelling over visual effects.
- Biblical fidelity over viral gimmicks.
- A believable shot over a flashy one.
- Better acting over more effects.
- Continuity over faster production.

The final result should feel like a coherent animated television series, not a collection of AI-generated clips.
